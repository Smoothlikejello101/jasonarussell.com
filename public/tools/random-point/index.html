    Before you head out

    • A random point is only a coordinate. It can land on private property, in water, on a highway, or somewhere that's unsafe to reach.
    • Check land ownership and access first. In the US, CalTopo's land management layer, onX, or the USGS PAD-US database show public land; elsewhere, use local land records.
    • Respect closures, posted signs, and hunting seasons. Get permission before entering private land, and redraw if the point isn't reachable.
    • These coordinates aren't navigation-grade. Carry a real map and let someone know where you're going.
    How the sampling works

    Picking a random direction and a random distance independently piles points up near the center, because the inner rings of a circle hold far less area than the outer ones. This tool instead chooses the distance so that every patch of ground inside the circle is equally likely.

    On a sphere, the area within angular distance d of the center is proportional to sin²(d/2). So with u uniform on [0, 1) and maximum angular radius D, the tool sets sin(d/2) = √u · sin(D/2). That's exact at any radius; for small circles it reduces to the familiar d = D·√u. The direction is uniform from 0° to 360°, and the point is placed along a great-circle path from the center. Randomness comes from your browser's cryptographic generator.

    The Earth is modeled as a sphere (radius 3,958.76 mi), which differs from the true ellipsoid by well under 1% — far smaller than any practical concern here.
